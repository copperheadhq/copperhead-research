A post on X<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> made the rounds with a tidy picture of a
differential pair going round a U-bend. The inner trace has less distance to cover, so it
arrives about 9.5 ps early. A serpentine of about 1.6 mm on that trace puts it back in
step.

The geometry is right. The step I wasn't sure about is the one every layout tool takes for
granted: equal length means equal delay. Length tuners that report in picoseconds still get
them from the length. A serpentine adds corners. It pulls one trace
away from its partner and folds back on itself, and none of that shows up in a length
report.

So I asked copperhead to check it with a field solver instead of a ruler.

## The bend is what geometry says

Two arcs that turn 180 degrees side by side differ in length by π times their spacing,
whatever the radius. At a 20 mil pitch that's ${dl} mm, or ${dl_mil} mil. Multiply by
√ε_eff and divide by the speed of light and you have the skew.

Which ε_eff you use matters. At 3.8 it comes to ${skew_38} ps. The post's 9.5 ps works
out to ε_eff = ${eps_95}, a perfectly normal number for FR-4 microstrip. copperhead's
stackup is 0.25 mm traces on 0.2 mm of ε_r 4.3, which has a single-line ε_eff of
${eps_single} by the Hammerstad–Jensen formula<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. So
geometry says ${skew_single} ps.

openEMS said ${skew_B} ps. For the bend on its own, geometry is within ${bend_err} % at a 50 ps edge.

## Seven boards that differ only in the tuning

copperhead built one fixture and changed nothing but the tuning. Every board has the same
stackup, the same launches and the same 12 mm legs.

C, D and F are the usual fix in three styles: two sparse excursions, a dense accordion of
four and one broad trombone. On each, copperhead solved the amplitude so that P and N come
out exactly the same length, chamfered corners included. C far is C moved next to the launch. E
is a control. It swings both traces out together, which adds delay to the pair and nothing
to the difference between them. KiCad's DRC passed every board before any of them went
into the solver.

## How copperhead simulates it

openEMS<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> solves Maxwell's equations around the copper
on a grid of cells. The board sits in a box: the copper on its dielectric, a perfect
conductor underneath as the return plane, and absorbing boundaries on the other five faces
so the fields leave instead of reflecting back.

::figure fig_model3d
alt: Board B's model in three dimensions, its height drawn four times true scale: the green dielectric slab on the PEC floor, the two gold traces, four red lumped ports from the floor to the copper at the launch, and the dashed edges of the air box whose five open faces are Mur boundaries.
caption: The model of board B. The height is drawn four times its true scale, or the slab would be a line.
::

Each run sends a pulse into one of the four ports, with the other three sitting there as
50 Ω loads<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. Four runs give the full response of the
board, its 4×4 S-matrix. scikit-rf<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> turns that into
differential and common-mode terms, and from there into what a receiver sees for a 50 ps
edge. All in, copperhead ran ${runs} simulations.

::figure fig_flow
alt: The pipeline as boxes in three groups. Geometry: the generator (geometry.py, solved lengths) and DRC (kicad-cli, P to N clearance). Stage III, full wave: the model (model.py, graded mesh), FDTD (openEMS, ${runs} runs) and S-parameters (sparams.py, 4-port, checks). Post-processing: mixed mode (scikit-rf, se2gmm), metrics (analysis.py, skew, TDR, eye) and convergence (convergence.py, error bounds). Under post-processing, Stage II: the line model (mtl.py, modes from A), fed from the mixed-mode step and read beside the metrics.
caption: From copper to waveforms. The line model sits beside the full-wave results, calibrated on the straight board.
::

The cells are ${cell_um} µm across over the copper and grow away from it, ${cells_m} million
of them for a bent board. The 45-degree corner cuts come out as staircases at that size,
which is the first thing to check.

So copperhead solved the untuned bend, two of the tunings and the serpentines again on
finer and coarser grids. On a ${fine_um} µm mesh the bend's skew moves by only
${conv_B_shift} ps. The bend's skew carries ±${unc_B} ps, C's ±${unc_C} ps and the dense
accordion's ±${unc_D} ps.

## Where the delay goes

The obvious suspect is the lengths. copperhead built a transmission-line model of every
board, calibrated on the solver's own straight pair so its coupled lines are the solver's
coupled lines. It knows every length to the micrometre and predicts ${mtl_F}, ${mtl_C} and
${mtl_D} ps for F, C and D. Length isn't what's missing.

What the line model can't see is the corners and the coupling between folds. To pull those
apart copperhead ran one more pair of boards. Each is a straight pair with 6 mm added to
one trace in three excursions, once with the folds 1.0 mm apart and once at the 0.15 mm
clearance rule. Same corners and same added length. They aren't quite the same board,
though. The tight one packs its folds into ${sec_tight} mm against ${sec_loose} mm, so P
also spends less of its run away from N.

Call the delay a tuning actually adds, over what its length says it should add, its
efficiency. The loose serpentine manages ${eta_Sloose}. Packed to the clearance rule it
drops to ${eta_Stight}, and it came out ${fold_pen_min} to ${fold_pen_max} points lower on
every mesh. That fits the fold coupling Wu and Chao<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup> and
Rubin and Singh<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> found in single-ended meanders. It doesn't
prove it, because of those section lengths.

The loose serpentine's own shortfall I'd put down to its corners, where the current cuts
across the inside of each turn. That path is shorter than the centre line the length report
measures. No board here changes the corners on their own, so that's my best guess rather
than a result.

::figure fig_corner

A copper line follows the path the current takes, pulled toward the inside of each of the four corners. Beside it, one corner at a larger scale, with the 45-degree chamfer's leg c = 75 micrometres dimensioned on the centreline.
caption: Why I think the corners cost delay. The current cuts the inside of each turn, so its path is shorter than the centre line.
:: In the bend fixes each excursion lost ${per_exc_F}, ${per_exc_C} and ${per_exc_D} ps of the
delay its length promised, for one, two and four excursions. The loss per excursion shrinks

lots of small excursions still does worst.

## Stripline didn't save it

Microstrip runs its two modes at different speeds, so some of this could have been a
microstrip quirk. copperhead put the same copper between two planes and ran it again, on
one mesh only.

It wasn't. Geometry predicts the stripline bend to ${bend_err_sl} % (${skew_Bsl} ps against
${skew_geo_sl} ps). The tunings removed ${removed_Fsl}, ${removed_Csl} and ${removed_Dsl} %
of the skew. In microstrip they removed ${removed_F}, ${removed_C} and ${removed_D} %.

Tight folds still cost ${fold_pen_sl} points. copperhead had written into the first draft of
its report that this penalty should shrink in stripline, because far-end coupling between
parallel lines vanishes there. It doesn't shrink. My best guess, which nothing here tests, is that neighbouring folds
carry current in opposite directions. The coupling that shortcuts them would then be
backward coupling, and stripline has plenty of that.

## Where the serpentine goes matters

C far is the same tuning as C, moved next to the launch. Its skew at the receiver isn't
much worse (${skew_Cfar} ps against ${skew_C} ps). Its mode conversion is. Between the
tuning and the bend the pair carries skew the other way, and the two corrections drift out
of phase as the frequency goes up. Read as a skew, C far's grows from ${fd_Cfar_5} ps at
5 GHz to ${fd_Cfar_25} ps at 25 GHz while C's stays flat. That's one placement against
another, not a sweep of the distance.

::figure fig_fd_skew

C next to the bend stays near ${skew_C} ps in teal. C at the launch rises from about ${fd_Cfar_5} ps at 5 GHz to about ${fd_Cfar_25} ps at 25 GHz in copper.
caption: The same tuning next to the bend and at the launch, read as a skew at each frequency.

## The trombone's bill

The broad trombone removed the most skew. But its trace leaves its partner for 3 mm and
the differential impedance there jumps to ${zmax_F} Ω, against ${zmax_B} Ω for the plain
bend. Its return loss is the worst of the length-matched boards (${sdd_F} dB against
${sdd_B} dB).

## What 9 ps does to a signal

At 16 Gb/s with 35 ps edges, the untuned bend closes the eye from ${eyeh_A} % to
${eyeh_B} % of the swing. That's small. The bigger effect is common mode, ${eyevc_B} % of
the swing peak to peak at the receiver against ${eyevc_F} % for the trombone. A receiver
with decent common-mode rejection won't notice the timing. Whether the common mode becomes
an emissions problem depends on the rest of the board, and none of this models that.

::figure fig_eye
alt: Two 16 Gb/s PRBS7 eyes with 35 ps edges, the straight pair at ${eyeh_A} % eye height and the untuned bend at ${eyeh_B} %, each with its common-mode voltage at the receiver underneath: flat for the straight pair and ${eyevc_B} % of the swing peak to peak for the bend.
caption: The eye barely moves. The common mode is where the skew shows.
::

## One more run fixes most of it

If each excursion loses a fixed amount, the fix is to put that amount back. copperhead
lengthened C, D and F by their own leftover skew, converted to length at the bend's rate.
By CAD measure P is now longer than N. The line model says all three overshoot: to
${mtl_Fcal} ps for F, ${mtl_Ccal} ps for C and ${mtl_Dcal} ps for D.

openEMS put F at ${skew_Fcal} ps and C at ${skew_Ccal} ps, so one iteration was enough for
those two. Neither was rerun on other meshes, so the ±${unc_C} ps from C's mesh check is a
loan here, not a measurement. The dense accordion only came down to ${skew_Dcal} ps. Each extra millimetre delivered
${marg_D} % of what it should have, against ${marg_C} % for C and ${marg_F} % for F. My
guess is that stretching its tightly packed verticals tightens the coupling between them
too.

## What I'd do on a real board

For edge-coupled pairs around this pitch, in microstrip or stripline:

- Trust geometry for the bend and not for the fix. Budget a few picoseconds of residual, or
  size the tuning from a field solve or a coupon.
- Use as few excursions as you can. The loss per excursion shrinks with its size, but not as fast as the count grows.
- Keep the folds apart. At the clearance rule they cost ${fold_pen_min} to ${fold_pen_max}
  points of efficiency.
- Tune next to the bend, not at the end of the route.
- Keep a broad excursion short or it'll show up on the TDR.
- Swinging the whole pair out does nothing for skew within the pair.

## Why copperhead runs studies like this

It's easy to build an AI for PCBs that's judged by the rules it follows. DRC passes, the
length report reads zero, done. On this bend that bar passes a board that still carries
${skew_D} ps of skew.

copperhead works the other way round. It treats a rule as a claim to test, runs the study
that tests it and keeps the result as a check with a number on it. This post is one of
those studies from start to finish. copperhead built the fixtures, ran the ${runs} solves,
caught its own mistakes (below) and wrote the report.

The checks run on real designs through fang<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup>,
copperhead's hardware kernel. A requirement that needs a simulation gets one. ngspice
takes the circuits and Renode runs the firmware against the board it's written for. A
value that breaks a requirement never reaches the design. One that nothing has measured
yet stays unknown instead of passing.

This study ran on its own scripts, beside fang rather than through it. fang can't ask a
field solver for skew yet, and the residual budget above is the first check I'd want it
to run.

## What went wrong on the way

The first runs never finished. openEMS stops once the energy in the model has decayed, and
here it stalled well short of the target. copperhead traced that to the DC content of the
pulse, which leaves a static field behind that never decays. Every run now stops at a fixed
${record_ns} ns instead, after checking that the signals at the ports have fallen at least
${trunc_db} dB.

The dense accordion wouldn't converge. On three meshes its skew came out at
${conv_D_coarse}, ${conv_D_medium} and ${conv_D_fine} ps. The honest bound on that is
±${gci_D_three} ps on a 5 ps result. Its 75 µm corner cuts are only a couple of cells wide.
A fourth, finer mesh gave ${conv_D_xfine} ps and settled it at about ${conv_D_ext} ps. The
coarse mesh had been the outlier.

It also first scored the stripline boards against microstrip's velocity, which made them
look ${sl_inflation} % better than they are. That got caught because the loose stripline
serpentine came out at ${eta_wrong_sl}, which didn't fit anything else in the study.

## What I still don't know

All of this is simulation. Nothing was built or measured. It's one trace width and one
pitch in one microstrip and one stripline stackup. The ports are lumped and the return
planes perfect. The copper has no roughness.

Every full-wave number here comes from one solver, openEMS. The line model was calibrated
on its runs, so the two agreeing isn't an independent check. Nor are the
mechanisms pinned down. The fold result is tangled up with section length, and the
corners are a guess by elimination.

The report has a coupon plan for checking it on real boards. The first thing I'd want to
see on a VNA is the dense accordion leaving ${skew_D} ps where the length report says zero.
